\begin{abstract}
Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) underpin models of financial markets, biological and physics systems, and robotics, yet identifying their dynamics from data is notoriously painful when only a handful of trajectories are available. Classical estimators and Neural SDEs alike typically assume long histories and require expensive re-training for every new system, which breaks down in realistic low-data regimes where each experiment, organism, or device produces only a few noisy paths. This work proposes a meta-learned Neural SDE that treats related SDEs as points on a continuous manifold of dynamics and learns a latent representation that can be rapidly adapted to new systems from just two trajectories. At test time, the method infers a task-specific latent code and performs few-shot fine-tuning, turning a small dataset from a single SDE into a useful forecasting tool rather than an underdetermined estimation problem. On a controlled family of basis-parametrised SDEs with interpolation, extrapolation, and out-of-distribution regimes, the meta-learner improves forecasting accuracy in data-starved environments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% \subsection{Motivation: Data-Scarce Stochastic Dynamics}
Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) are a standard language for modeling complex systems under uncertainty, from financial volatility and interest rate dynamics to noisy physical and biological processes \cite{oksendal2003, gardiner2009}. In these models, the drift encodes deterministic trends while the diffusion captures stochastic fluctuations, providing a principled way to describe continuous-time dynamics driven by both physics and noise.

Despite their ubiquity, system identification for SDEs, recovering drift and diffusion from observed trajectories is notoriously difficult when data are scarce. Classical estimators based on likelihoods or the Fokker--Planck equation typically require long time horizons and dense sampling to reliably disentangle deterministic and stochastic effects, and they are sensitive to discretisation and model mismatch \cite{kloeden1992, sarkka2019}. In many realistic settings, however, only a handful of short, noisy trajectories are available for any given system.

Neural SDEs promise a more flexible alternative by parameterising drift and diffusion with neural networks \cite{tzen2019, kidger2021}. In principle, a suitably expressive Neural SDE can approximate a wide range of stochastic dynamics without imposing a rigid functional form. In practice, though, most Neural SDE applications are single-task: each new system is treated as an independent problem, and a fresh model is trained from scratch on that system's trajectories. This ``tabula rasa'' approach is data- and compute-intensive and ignores the fact that many systems are variations of a common physical template: changing parameters such as friction, stiffness, or volatility often moves the dynamics along a continuum rather than defining an entirely unrelated process.

% \subsection{From Discrete Transfer to a Continuous Task Manifold}
Meta-learning, or ``learning to learn,'' provides a natural way to exploit such cross-task structure. In few-shot learning, meta-learners are trained across many related tasks so that they can adapt quickly to new tasks from only a few examples \cite{finn2017, nichol2018}. Most existing work, however, has focused on discrete task families (e.g., classification over different label sets) or deterministic dynamical systems \citepalias{li2023prl, li2023adjoint}, and relatively little work has explored meta-learning for stochastic dynamics in continuous time.

A key observation in our setting is that tasks are not arbitrary labels but points in a continuous parameter space. In our data generation process, each SDE is defined by a parameter vector $\theta$ that linearly combines a fixed set of basis functions for the drift and diffusion; different choices of $\theta$ yield different but related SDEs, and moving $\theta$ smoothly changes the dynamics. Rather than learning separate models for each system or transferring between a small set of discrete ``equations,'' we aim to meta-learn over this continuous, basis-parametrised manifold of tasks.
 

We therefore reframe few-shot SDE system identification as meta-learning over a continuous task manifold. Instead of asking ``can we transfer from one named SDE to another?'', we ask ``can we learn a global stochastic dynamics model that, when conditioned on a low-dimensional latent code, approximates an entire family of SDEs, and can we infer the latent code of a new system from only a few short trajectories?''

\subsection{Our Approach: Latent-Conditioned Neural SDEs}
To answer this question, we propose a meta-learning framework built around a latent-conditioned Neural SDE. At its core is a Neural SDE whose drift and diffusion networks take as input both the current state and a task-specific latent vector $z$. A trajectory encoder processes short prefixes of observed trajectories and produces an initial estimate of $z$. During meta-training, we expose this encoder--Neural-SDE--head architecture to trajectories from many SDEs sampled from the basis-parametrised family, and we optimize all components jointly so that $z$ becomes a meaningful representation of where a task lies on the underlying dynamics manifold (\citealp{rusu2018}; \citealp{finn2017}).
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{framework_diagram.png}\vss
    \caption{Overview of our Meta-SDE framework. During meta-training, a latent-conditioned Neural SDE is trained across tasks to learn a shared stochastic prior over dynamics. At meta-test time, a small support set of trajectories is encoded into a latent variable $z$, which is refined by optimisation while the global dynamics remain fixed. Predictions are generated by solving the Neural SDE and are optionally mixed with a conservative prior when adaptation residuals indicate high uncertainty.}
    \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}


At meta-test time, each new task is defined by an unseen parameter vector $\theta$, but we only observe a small support set of trajectories from that system. We treat these support trajectories as completed historical experiments. The encoder converts early-time segments into an initial latent code, which we further refine by optimising only the latent to match the observed past under the meta-trained Neural SDE, while keeping the global dynamics parameters fixed. A lightweight forecasting head, conditioned on the refined latent and the Neural SDE's terminal state, is optionally fine-tuned on the same short history to calibrate the output. Evaluation metrics are computed on disjoint query trajectories, ensuring no leakage between adaptation and testing.

This design follows the representation-based meta-learning perspective, where most adaptation is pushed into a low-dimensional representation and a small readout, rather than constantly re-training a high-capacity backbone \cite{rusu2018, yu2022}. For stochastic dynamics, this is particularly important: aggressively fine-tuning the SDE parameters on very short, noisy trajectories can lead to overfitting and negative transfer, whereas adapting a latent code and readout is more stable and better aligned with the structure of the meta-training objective \cite{yosinski2014}. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarises the framework. Tasks correspond to points on a continuous manifold of stochastic dynamics, while the Neural SDE defines a shared prior over this space. Few-shot adaptation is performed by inferring a low-dimensional latent coordinate from short trajectories rather than re-training the dynamics model. This perspective underlies the formal development in Section~3.


\subsection*{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}

We frame the problem as \emph{few-shot stochastic dynamics learning} rather than isolated SDE fitting: a task is an unknown point on a continuous manifold of dynamics (\cref{sec:manifold}), and adaptation is primarily an \emph{inference problem} over a low-dimensional latent code (\cref{sec:framework,sec:adapt}). Our method learns a latent-conditioned Neural SDE that functions as a meta-learned prior over drift and diffusion (\cref{sec:framework}), together with a lightweight adaptation interface that updates only the latent and a small readout while freezing the dynamics backbone (\cref{sec:arch}). We introduce a basis-parametrised benchmark with explicit interpolation, extrapolation, and out-of-distribution regimes (\cref{sec:manifold,sec:sampling_regimes}), and we evaluate sample efficiency and failure modes via data-efficiency curves, residual-gated fallbacks, and regime-switch stress tests (\cref{sec:protocol,sec:results}).


\section{Related Work}

Related work on our topic spans neural differential equations, meta-learning and few-shot adaptation, and system identification with parametric structure.

\textbf{Neural differential equations.}
Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) model deep networks as continuous-time flows, replacing discrete residual blocks with an ODE solver and enabling adjoint-based training with constant memory \cite{chen2018neural}. These models sparked a line of work treating differential equation solvers as learnable components and using continuous-time parameterisations for sequence modeling and generative modeling. Neural SDEs extend this idea to stochastic dynamics by parameterising both drift and diffusion with neural networks, with Tzen and Raginsky \yrcite{tzen2019} providing a diffusion-limit view as deep latent Gaussian models and Kidger et al. \yrcite{kidger2021} introducing efficient and accurate gradient estimators for Neural SDEs that make training such models practical. Our approach adopts this Neural SDE perspective, but instead of fitting a separate model per system, we train a single latent-conditioned Neural SDE across many systems and focus on few-shot adaptation in the latent space rather than per-dataset training from scratch.

\textbf{Meta-learning and few-shot adaptation.}
Meta-learning methods such as Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) learn an initialisation that can be quickly adapted to new tasks by a few gradient steps on task-specific data \cite{finn2017}. First-order variants reduce computational cost and show that full second-order gradients are often unnecessary in practice \cite{nichol2018}. In contrast to these gradient-based parameter-initialisation methods, representation-based approaches like Latent Embedding Optimization (LEO) learn a low-dimensional latent representation of task-specific parameters and perform adaptation in this latent space, which can be more stable and data-efficient in high-dimensional models \cite{rusu2018}. Neural Processes similarly encode sets of context points into a latent summary used to predict function values at target points, emphasising context-based adaptation via a global representation rather than direct gradient steps on all weights \cite{garnelo2018}. Our method is closer to these representation-based approaches: we meta-learn a shared Neural SDE backbone and adapt only a low-dimensional latent code and a lightweight readout, rather than fine-tuning the entire dynamics network for each new SDE.

\textbf{Meta-learning for dynamical systems.}
Several works bring meta-learning ideas to dynamical systems. \citetalias{li2023prl} propose a framework for meta-learning generalizable dynamics from trajectories, demonstrating that identifying shared physical structures across tasks enables rapid adaptation to new deterministic environments. Similarly, \citetalias{li2023adjoint} study meta-learning with adjoint methods for neural differential equations to improve gradient stability and efficiency. However, these methods primarily address deterministic systems or focus on recovering the drift component of the dynamics. In contrast, our work targets stochastic differential equations where both drift and diffusion are task-dependent, and we explicitly meta-learn a prior over stochastic dynamics such that both mean behavior and noise statistics can be adapted via a task-specific latent code inferred from short, noisy trajectories.

\textbf{System identification and basis-parametrised dynamics.}
Classical system identification for dynamical systems often assumes a parametric structure and attempts to recover governing equations from data. Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) uses a library of candidate basis functions and sparse regression to discover compact ODE models directly from trajectories \cite{brunton2016}. In stochastic settings, Särkkä and Solin \yrcite{sarkka2019} provide a comprehensive treatment of SDE modeling and inference, including maximum-likelihood and filtering-based approaches that assume specific parametric forms for drift and diffusion. Our setup is inspired by this parametric viewpoint but uses the basis expansion only in the data generator, where each task corresponds to an SDE whose drift and diffusion are linear combinations of fixed basis functions with different coefficients. On the learning side, we do not attempt to recover a sparse symbolic equation; instead, we train a Neural SDE that learns a smooth latent manifold over these parameterized dynamics and performs few-shot adaptation by inferring where a new system lies on this manifold from a small number of short trajectories.

\textbf{Representation transfer and selective adaptation.}
Work on transfer learning has shown that some layers of deep networks transfer better than others and that blindly fine-tuning all parameters on limited data can harm performance, especially under distribution shift \cite{yosinski2014}. Recent studies on representation transfer for few-shot learning further emphasize that adapting only a small head or a low-dimensional representation on top of a shared backbone can be more effective and robust than full-network fine-tuning, particularly when target data are scarce \cite{yu2022}. These insights motivate our design choice to freeze the meta-trained Neural SDE backbone during adaptation and restrict few-shot learning to a latent code and forecasting head, which our experiments show is sufficient to capture task-specific behavior while avoiding the instability associated with re-training the full stochastic dynamics model on only a few short trajectories.

\section{Mathematical Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{The Basis-Parametrised Task Manifold}
\label{sec:manifold}
We consider families of dynamical systems over a continuous parameter space $\Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^P$. Each $\theta \in \Theta$ generates trajectories via a parametric It\^o SDE:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t = b(X_t; \theta)\,dt + \sigma(X_t; \theta)\,dW_t.
    \label{eq:parametric_sde}
\end{equation}

For vector-valued states $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, basis functions are applied component-wise. That is, for a scalar basis function $\tilde\phi_j$,
\[
\phi_j(x)_\ell = \tilde\phi_j(x_\ell), \qquad \ell = 1,\dots,d.
\]
Drift and diffusion bases are constructed independently for each state dimension, yielding diagonal dynamics.


Throughout this work, we consider state vectors $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and Brownian motion $W_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The diffusion term is assumed to be diagonal, with $G_\phi(x,t,z)$ outputting a $d$-dimensional vector that is interpreted as a diagonal diffusion matrix via $\mathrm{diag}(G_\phi(x,t,z))$. This simplifies modelling and is sufficient for the benchmarks considered.


\subsection{Latent-Conditioned Neural SDE}
\label{sec:latent_sde}
The meta-model approximates \eqref{eq:parametric_sde} via a single Neural SDE:
\begin{equation}
    dX_t = f_\phi(X_t,z)\,dt + \mathbf{D}(g_\phi(X_t,z))\,dW_t,
    \label{eq:meta_sde}
\end{equation}
$f_\phi, g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^d$ are MLPs, and $\mathbf{D}(v) = \text{diag}(v)$.

Our analysis relies on standard conditions for existence and uniqueness of solutions to latent-conditioned SDEs. In particular, under global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions on the learned drift and diffusion, the Neural SDE admits a unique strong solution (Assumption~A.1). Moreover, small perturbations of the latent coordinate induce bounded changes in trajectories (Proposition~A.2), supporting the interpretation of $z$ as a continuous manifold coordinate.

\subsection{Meta-objective and adaptation}
\label{sec:adapt}

\textbf{Meta-training.}
Given a task with trajectories $X_{0:T}$, the encoder maps a short prefix to a latent code $z=r_\psi(X_{0:M})$, and the latent-conditioned Neural SDE generates a full reconstructed path $\hat X_{0:T}$ via numerical integration of \cref{eq:meta_sde}. We train the encoder, SDE parameters, and a lightweight head jointly by minimising a reconstruction term plus an auxiliary forecast term,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:meta_objective}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{meta}}
=
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{X}_{0:T}, X_{0:T})
+
\lambda\,\mathrm{MSE}\!\big(h_\omega(\hat{X}_T, z), X_{T+\Delta}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda=0.1$ in all experiments (\cref{sec:protocol}). The forecast term encourages the latent to carry decision-relevant information for prediction beyond the reconstruction horizon.
\newline
\textbf{Few-shot adaptation as inference.}
At meta-test time, a new task is specified only through a tiny support set $\mathcal{D}_s=\{X^{(i)}_{0:M}\}_{i=1}^{K}$ with $K=2$ trajectories. We initialise $z$ using the encoder, then refine it by optimising only the task-specific latent and head parameters while keeping the backbone $(f_\phi,g_\phi)$ fixed. Concretely, we solve
\begin{align}
\nonumber
(z^\star,\omega^\star)
=
&\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z,\omega}
\sum_{i=1}^{K}
\Big(
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat X^{(i)}_{0:T},X^{(i)}_{0:T})
\\&\quad+
\|h_\omega(\hat X_T^{(i)},z)-X_{T+\Delta}^{(i)}\|_2^2
\Big)
+
\beta\|z\|_2^2,\label{eq:adapt_regularized}
\end{align}
where $\beta=10^{-3}$ and $\hat X^{(i)}$ is obtained by integrating \ref{eq:meta_sde} from $X_0^{(i)}$ under latent $z$. The $\ell_2$ penalty constrains inference to remain near the meta-learned manifold when support evidence is weak, and it is this bias that later enables graceful degradation under distribution shift (\cref{sec:gating,sec:results}).

Finally, we compute the normalised residual $D_{\mathrm{res}}$ in \cref{eq:dres} and mix predictions from the adapted latent and a conservative default latent:
\begin{align}\nonumber
\hat X^{\mathrm{query}}
=
(1-g)\,\mathrm{NeuralSDE}(X_0^{\mathrm{query}},0)&
\\+
g\,\mathrm{NeuralSDE}(X_0^{\mathrm{query}},z^\star)&,\label{eq:mixture}
\end{align}
where $g=\sigma(\tilde g)$ and $\tilde g$ is the gate logit defined in \cref{sec:gating}. This makes the failure mode explicit: when adaptation cannot explain the support set, the method retreats to the meta-learned prior rather than hallucinating task-specific structure.

\subsection{Residual-based gating (safety reflex)}
\label{sec:gating}
\vspace{-0.5ex}
We detect misadaptation using a normalised support residual
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dres}
D_{\mathrm{res}}
=\frac{1}{K(T+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{t=0}^{T}
\frac{\|\hat X^{(i)}_t - X^{(i)}_t\|_2^2}{\|X^{(i)}_{0:T}\|_2^2+\epsilon},
\quad \epsilon=10^{-8}.
\end{equation}
We convert this statistic to a gate via $\tilde g=\alpha(\tau-D_{\mathrm{res}})$ and $g=\sigma(\tilde g)$, so large residuals reduce reliance on the adapted latent and increase reliance on the conservative prior in \ref{eq:mixture}.


\section{Architecture} \label{sec:arch} \cref{sec:framework} defines the model as an inference-and-adaptation procedure on a task manifold. This section records the concrete parameterisations used to instantiate that procedure, focusing on what is necessary to reproduce the results rather than re-stating the mathematical setup. This modular design enables the system to transfer general physical laws (via the frozen SDE) while rapidly specialising to specific local dynamics (via the head) using minimal data. Below, we detail the specifications of each component and the end-to-end computational flow.


\subsection{Context Encoder}
\vspace{-0.5ex}
The encoder $r_\psi$ maps a prefix $X_{0:M}$ to $z\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ with a 2-layer GRU (hidden 256, dropout 0.1); we use the final hidden state and a linear projection. Unless stated otherwise, $M=50$ and $d=10$.


\subsection{Latent-conditioned Neural SDE}
\vspace{-0.5ex}
We instantiate \cref{eq:meta_sde} with two MLPs $f_\phi,g_\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d+16}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$, each using two 128-unit SiLU layers (LayerNorm after linear layers). To enforce non-negative diagonal diffusion, we set
\[
g_\phi(x,z)=\mathrm{Softplus}(\tilde g_\phi(x,z)) + 10^{-3}.
\]
We integrate with Euler--Maruyama ($\Delta t=0.005$, $T=1.0$, 200 steps) and apply a soft clamp $X_t\in[-10,10]^d$ for stability.


\subsection{Forecasting head and optimisation}
\vspace{-0.5ex}
The head $h_\omega:\mathbb{R}^{d+16}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a 2-layer 128-unit MLP. At test time we optimise $(z,\omega)$ for 50 Adam steps (lr $10^{-2}$) under \cref{eq:adapt_regularized} while freezing $(\phi,\psi)$. We backpropagate directly through the solver (BPTT); with 200 steps and $d\le 17$, the graph fits in GPU memory.


\section{The Basis-Parametrised Data Manifold}
\vspace{-0.5ex}
Our synthetic benchmark instantiates the continuous task manifold already defined in \cref{sec:manifold}: each task is an It\^o SDE whose drift and diagonal diffusion are linear combinations of fixed scalar bases. We evaluate interpolation, extrapolation (mean shift), and OOD (mean+variance shift) by sampling $\theta$ from three held-out distributions over $\Theta$ (Test A/B/C), enabling controlled generalisation as a function of distance in coefficient space.


\subsection{Classical SDEs as points in $\Theta$}
\label{sec:classical_points}

Several standard processes appear as sparse corners of the basis space. For example, geometric Brownian motion corresponds to activating the linear coefficient in the drift together with the linear term in the diffusion; an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process arises by using an affine drift (constant plus linear term) with near-constant diffusion; and double-well behaviour emerges when cubic and quartic drift terms dominate. This viewpoint matters because it makes interpolation and extrapolation meaningful: moving in coefficient space continuously morphs one process into another, allowing us to evaluate generalisation as a function of distance in $\Theta$ rather than as a discrete jump between named equations (\cref{sec:sampling_regimes,sec:results}).


\subsection{Sampling regimes}
\label{sec:sampling_regimes}

We evaluate meta-generalisation by sampling tasks from three held-out distributions over $\Theta$. Test A probes interpolation by sampling from the same coefficient distribution as meta-training. Test B applies a mild mean shift to move tasks outside the convex hull of the training set. Test C applies a larger shift and variance rescaling that emphasises higher-order drift terms and harder diffusion structure, producing strongly out-of-distribution dynamics. These regimes are used throughout \cref{sec:protocol,sec:results} and provide an explicit knob on uncertainty: the further the shift, the more adaptation should rely on conservative behaviour through the gate (\cref{sec:gating}).

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We use $N_{\mathrm{tasks}}$ to denote the number of meta-training tasks, $K$ to denote the number of support trajectories per task (shots), and $N_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ when explicitly varying the number of context trajectories in data-efficiency studies.
\newline
\textbf{Metrics and failure modes.}
We report (i) \emph{open-loop rollout MSE}, the mean squared error over the full predicted trajectory when the model is rolled forward without teacher forcing; (ii) \emph{final-step MSE} at $T+\Delta$, which isolates endpoint forecasting; and (iii) the \emph{support residual} $D_{\mathrm{res}}$ in \cref{eq:dres}, which is the statistic used by the gate. The key failure mode in the few-shot regime is not merely higher error but \emph{catastrophic mis-adaptation}: a small support set can drive the model towards a spurious latent that fits noise and degrades rollout predictions. The gating mechanism is evaluated specifically on its ability to prevent this failure under Test C shifts (\cref{sec:sampling_regimes,sec:results}).


To evaluate data efficiency across the manifold defined in Section 5, we generate a fixed universe of tasks sampled from the parameter space $\Theta$. The meta-training set $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ consists of $N=150$ distinct dynamical systems sampled from the training distribution. For each system, we simulate 80 trajectories for inner-loop updates and 20 for validation. Evaluation is performed on three held-out test sets ($N=30$ tasks each) corresponding to the regimes defined previously: Test A (Interpolation), Test B (Mild Extrapolation), and Test C (OOD/Chaos). All synthetic trajectories are simulated with high precision ($dt=0.005$) over a horizon of $T=1.0$ ($200$ steps) and standardized to zero mean and unit variance.


\subsection{Meta-Training Implementation}
The meta-model is trained end-to-end by minimising the meta-objective in \cref{eq:meta_objective}.
We utilise the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of $\eta=10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 64 trajectories. Training proceeds for 50 epochs with global gradient clipping (max norm 1.0) to stabilize the optimisation of chaotic vector fields. The objective function balances trajectory reconstruction with forecasting accuracy:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{meta}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{head}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}$ is the MSE over the full path $X_{0:T}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{head}}$ is the error at $T+\Delta$. We set $\lambda=0.1$ to prioritize the learning of the underlying physics over pure endpoint regression. Crucially, we employ Context Randomisation during training, where the encoder observes a random prefix length $M \sim \mathcal{U}[20, 50]$. This stochasticity prevents the encoder from overfitting to a fixed context window, forcing it to learn robust features from varying amounts of history.
\newline\textbf{Few-shot adaptation protocol.} For each test task, we sample $K=2$ support trajectories, initialise $z$ by mean-pooling encoder outputs, and optimise \cref{eq:adapt_regularized} for 50 Adam steps at learning rate $10^{-2}$ while freezing $(\phi,\psi)$. We then compute $D_{\mathrm{res}}$ using \eqref{eq:dres} and form query predictions with the mixture rule \eqref{eq:mixture}. All hyperparameters ($\beta,\alpha,\tau$) are fixed globally and are not tuned per regime to avoid leaking test difficulty into the method.
\newline\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare against four baselines that separate architectural benefit from meta-learning benefit. \emph{Scratch} trains a reduced-capacity Neural SDE on the support set only, testing whether few-shot learning is possible without a learned prior. \emph{Transfer} trains a single global Neural SDE with fixed latent $z=0$ on all meta-training data and fine-tunes on the support set, testing whether a generic initialisation suffices without task inference. \emph{MAML} performs first-order meta-learning and adapts model weights directly from a meta-initialisation, testing parameter-space adaptation under data scarcity. Finally, a \emph{probabilistic GRU} is trained as a discrete-time sequence model and fine-tuned on the support set, testing whether continuous-time structure is necessary for long-horizon robustness.


\subsection{Continuous Control Benchmarks}
To evaluate our framework's capability for System Identification and OOD Generalisation in high-dimensional, non-linear environments, we use the DeepMind Control Suite (DMCS)~\cite{tassa2018deepmind}. We construct a ``Virtual Robot Lab'' paradigm where the meta-model must adapt to robotic systems with unknown physical properties (e.g., mass and friction) using only sparse observations ($2$ trajectories). All robotic experiments are in the DMCS.
\newline
\textbf{Environments.}
We use Reacher ($D=6$) as a controlled diagnostic, Finger Spin ($D=9$) as a contact-rich system with discontinuities, and Cheetah Run ($D=17$) as a high-dimensional stress test. This spans smooth dynamics, mode switches, and under-actuated motion, allowing us to test if the latent prior remains usable as complexity increases.
\newline
\textbf{Physics randomisation and regimes.}
Each episode randomises body mass and geom friction relative to defaults. Meta-training uses 50 configurations with scalings in $[0.8,1.2]$. Test A samples new seeds from the same range. Test B shifts to heavier and more slippery systems (Mass in $[1.5,2.0]$, Friction in $[0.5,0.8]$). Test C applies an extreme shift (Mass in $[2.0,3.0]$, Friction in $[0.1,0.5]$) intended to trigger gating and expose failure modes.
\newline
\textbf{Data Protocol.}
Trajectories are generated with a horizon of $T=200$ steps. The adaptation protocol remains strictly few-shot ($N$-way, $K$-shot with $K=2$), ensuring the model must infer the system parameters $z$ almost instantaneously without extensive re-training.

\subsection{Online Adaptation: Regime Switching}
To evaluate the model's robustness to non-stationary dynamics (sudden changes in the underlying physics during operation), we simulate a ``Regime Switch'' scenario. We construct a composite trajectory by concatenating the first 50 timesteps of a Task A trajectory with a trajectory from a distinct Task B. The model processes this stream using a sliding-window encoder ($M=20$). At timestep $t=50$, the physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction) change abruptly. We measure the system's plasticity via two specific metrics:

\textbf{Peak Shock Error:} Maximum instantaneous prediction error immediately following regime switches, representing the model's sensitivity to violations of current physical beliefs.
\newline
\textbf{Recovery Time:} Number of timesteps required for the rolling encoder to discard the old context, infer the new latent code $z_{\text{new}}$, and return the prediction error to within a $2.0\times$ threshold of the baseline accuracy.

This experiment tests whether the Continuous-Time SDE can leverage the encoder's varying context window to seamlessly transition between dynamical modes without requiring manual reset or re-training.

\subsection{Data Efficiency: Few-Shot Adaptation Scaling}
To quantify the model's ability to aggregate information from sparse observations, we evaluate the prediction performance as a function of the support set size. We vary the number of context trajectories $N \in \{1, 2, 5, 10\}$. For a given task $\mathcal{T}_i$, we sample $N$ support trajectories $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \dots, \mathbf{x}^{(N)}$. The encoder processes the first $T=20$ timesteps of each trajectory to produce individual latent codes $z^{(n)}$. These are aggregated via a mean-pooling operation to produce a robust task embedding $\bar{z}$:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{z} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \text{Encoder}(\mathbf{x}^{(n)}_{1:20})
\end{equation}
This aggregated code $\bar{z}$ is then used to condition the Neural SDE for inference on a distinct, held-out query trajectory from the same task. We measure the \textit{One-Step MSE} on the query set to determine the marginal gain in predictive accuracy provided by each additional context rollout.


\subsection{Ablation Studies}
To validate design choices, we ablate key components of the Gated Regularized framework. These variations correspond directly to the models detailed in Appendix A.
\newline
\textbf{1. Unregularised Adaptation (Model B):} This variant performs joint optimisation of $z$ and forecast head $\omega$, but removes the regularisation term ($\beta=0$ in Eq. \ref{eq:adapt_regularized}). This tests the hypothesis that without the anchored prior (the KL/$\ell_2$ penalty), the latent variable is prone to overfitting or catastrophic divergence in chaotic OOD regimes (Test C).
\newline
\textbf{2. Head-Only Adaptation (Model A):} In this baseline, the latent encoder is frozen ($z = z_{\text{init}}$), and only the forecast head parameters $\omega$ are adapted to the support set. This isolates the benefit of \textit{latent plasticity}, testing whether adapting the manifold coordinate $z$ is necessary or if adapting the readout mapping $\omega$ is sufficient.
\newline
These ablations isolate the contributions of (1) latent regularisation for safety and (2) latent plasticity for adaptation. The results (detailed in Appendix A.1) confirm that the full regularized method (Model C) achieves the optimal plasticity-stability trade-off.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate (i) few-shot sample efficiency, (ii) robustness under Test B/C shifts, and (iii) non-stationarity under regime switching. Metrics and protocols follow \cref{sec:protocol}; all results average over held-out tasks. For completeness, we provide full per-regime tables (including gate statistics, residuals, and timing) for all methods in Appendix~B--C, and additional analyses/ablations in Appendix~A.

As detailed in Appendix B, the MAML baseline saturates at a high error ($1.46$), suggesting the meta-learned initialisation fails to generalise to the shifted loss landscape of the OOD tasks. \subsection{Ablations}
Head-only adaptation (Model A) can match or slightly improve Test A error but degrades faster under Test B/C shifts, consistent with insufficient task inference. Unregularised latent adaptation (Model B) is strongest in-distribution but exhibits latent drift under Test C (gate collapses at long horizons). Regularisation (Model C) yields the most reliable plasticity–stability trade-off across regimes.
\newline
\textbf{Impact of Latent Plasticity (Model A vs. C):}
Model A (Head-Only adaptation) effectively freezes the latent code, relying solely on the readout layer. While stable, it underperforms on the in-distribution Test A regime (MSE $0.149$ vs. $0.163$ for Model C), indicating the fixed latent code hinders fine-tuning to specific dynamical nuances.
\newline
\textbf{Impact of Regularisation (Model B vs. C):}
Model B (Unregularised) achieves the lowest error on Test A ($0.148$) by allowing the latent code to drift freely. This compromises safety in OOD settings. In the chaotic Test C regime (Table \ref{tab:model_b}), Model B's Gate Value collapses to approx. $0.005$ at long horizons, indicating a total loss of confidence. Model C, constrained by the $\ell_2$ penalty, maintains a better-conditioned latent space, offering an optimal trade-off between the plasticity of Model B and the stability of Model A.

\subsection{Scalability to High-Dimensional Robotics}
We evaluate our framework's scalability using the DMCS. 
\newline\textbf{Interpreting rollout versus endpoint error.}
In the DMCS, trajectories are standardised and evaluation horizons are short, so open-loop rollout error can appear small even when prediction quality is imperfect. We therefore report endpoint error as a more decision-relevant statistic for downstream use. Across regimes, the gate logit decreases under larger physics shifts, reflecting reduced confidence in the adapted latent and increased reliance on the conservative prior (Section~3.4).

We further stress-test the model's plasticity via the Regime Switch experiment, where the physical parameters change abruptly at $t=50$.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a framework for few-shot stochastic dynamics learning that treats SDE estimation as inference over a meta-learned manifold of dynamics rather than per-task parameter fitting. A latent-conditioned Neural SDE acts as a shared stochastic prior, while task-specific adaptation is restricted to a low-dimensional latent code and a lightweight readout. This separation enables stable and sample-efficient adaptation from extremely limited trajectory data. Experiments on a continuous basis-parametrised SDE benchmark and on high-dimensional robotic systems demonstrate improved forecasting accuracy and robustness compared to from-scratch training, warm-start transfer, and gradient-based meta-learning. Crucially, the residual-based gating mechanism makes failure modes explicit: when the support set is insufficient or out-of-distribution, the model degrades conservatively by reverting to the learned prior rather than overfitting spurious dynamics.

The approach has limitations. Numerical SDE solvers incur higher computational cost than discrete-time models, and the meta-training distribution is restricted to a chosen basis family, which does not cover all stochastic systems, particularly those with discontinuities or jumps. The gating mechanism is heuristic and uses residual error as a proxy for epistemic uncertainty rather than a calibrated posterior measure. Overall, the work advances reusable priors for stochastic system identification under severe data scarcity. The emphasis on conservative behaviour under uncertainty is intended to support safe deployment in adaptive and safety-critical settings, while acknowledging the potential for misuse in sensitive domains if overconfident inference were allowed.
\clearpage
\section*{Impact Statement}

This research advances the field of safety-critical system identification. By enabling robots and automated systems to adapt rapidly to changing physical conditions (e.g., wear-and-tear, payload changes) using only sparse data, our methods could improve the reliability of autonomous deployment in unstructured environments. However, we acknowledge the dual-use potential of generative dynamics models. The same capability to infer underlying physics from few observations could theoretically be applied to infer sensitive properties of proprietary physical systems or to model financial volatility for high-frequency trading in ways that exacerbate market instability. We emphasize the use of the \textit{Safety Reflex} mechanism specifically to mitigate risks, ensuring that automated systems default to conservative behaviors when faced with uncertain or adversarial dynamics.

\section{Theoretical Considerations and Assumptions}
\label{app:theory}

This appendix distinguishes formal guarantees from modelling assumptions and implementation heuristics.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{assumption}[Well-posed Neural SDE]
\label{assump:wellposed}
For any fixed latent code $z \in \mathcal{Z}$, the functions $f_\phi(\cdot,\cdot,z)$ and $G_\phi(\cdot,\cdot,z)$ satisfy global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions in the state variable. Under this assumption, the Neural SDE in Equation~\eqref{eq:meta_sde} admits a unique strong solution.
\end{assumption}
\subsection{Theoretical Results}

\begin{proposition}[Stability under small latent perturbations]
\label{prop:latent_stability}
Assume the latent-conditioned drift and diffusion $(x,z)\mapsto f_\phi(x,z)$ and $(x,z)\mapsto g_\phi(x,z)$ satisfy, for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $z,z'\in\mathcal{Z}$,
\begin{align}
\|f_\phi(x,z)-f_\phi(y,z')\|_2 &\le L_x\|x-y\|_2 + L_z\|z-z'\|_2, \label{eq:lipschitz_fxz}\\
\|g_\phi(x,z)-g_\phi(y,z')\|_2 &\le L_x\|x-y\|_2 + L_z\|z-z'\|_2, \label{eq:lipschitz_gxz}
\end{align}
and the usual linear growth condition ensuring existence of a unique strong solution on $[0,T]$.
Let $X_t(z)$ and $X_t(z')$ be solutions to \eqref{eq:meta_sde} driven by the same Brownian motion and the same initial condition $X_0$.
Then there exists a constant $C>0$ depending only on $(T,L_x,L_z)$ such that
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|X_t(z)-X_t(z')\|_2^2\Big] \le C\,\|z-z'\|_2^2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Delta_t := X_t(z)-X_t(z')$. Then
\[
\Delta_t = \int_0^t \big(f_\phi(X_s(z),z)-f_\phi(X_s(z'),z')\big)\,ds
+ \int_0^t \big(g_\phi(X_s(z),z)-g_\phi(X_s(z'),z')\big)\,dW_s.
\]
Using $(a+b)^2\le 2a^2+2b^2$ and Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
\sup_{u\le t}\|\Delta_u\|_2^2 \;\le\;
2t\int_0^t \|f_\phi(X_s(z),z)-f_\phi(X_s(z'),z')\|_2^2 ds
+ 2\sup_{u\le t}\Big\|\int_0^u \Gamma_s\,dW_s\Big\|_2^2,
\]
where $\Gamma_s:=g_\phi(X_s(z),z)-g_\phi(X_s(z'),z')$.
Taking expectations and applying the Burkholder--Davis--Gundy inequality to the martingale term yields
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\le t}\|\Delta_u\|_2^2\Big]
\le
2t\int_0^t \mathbb{E}\|F_s\|_2^2 ds
+ C_{\mathrm{BDG}}\int_0^t \mathbb{E}\|\Gamma_s\|_2^2 ds,
\]
with $F_s:=f_\phi(X_s(z),z)-f_\phi(X_s(z'),z')$.
Using the Lipschitz bounds \eqref{eq:lipschitz_fxz}--\eqref{eq:lipschitz_gxz},
\[
\mathbb{E}\|F_s\|_2^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\Gamma_s\|_2^2
\le
2L_x^2\,\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_s\|_2^2 + 2L_z^2\|z-z'\|_2^2
\le
2L_x^2\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\le s}\|\Delta_u\|_2^2\Big] + 2L_z^2\|z-z'\|_2^2.
\]
Therefore, for some constant $A>0$ depending on $(T,L_x,C_{\mathrm{BDG}})$ and some $B>0$ depending on $(T,L_z,C_{\mathrm{BDG}})$,
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\le t}\|\Delta_u\|_2^2\Big]
\le
A\int_0^t \mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{u\le s}\|\Delta_u\|_2^2\Big] ds
+ B\,\|z-z'\|_2^2.
\]
An application of Gr\"onwall's inequality gives
\[
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\Delta_t\|_2^2\Big] \le B e^{AT}\,\|z-z'\|_2^2,
\]
which proves the claim.
The argument follows the standard stability line used in existence and uniqueness proofs for SDEs under global Lipschitz assumptions; see, e.g., the estimates derived in \citet[Ch.~5]{oksendal2003}, \citet[Prop~4.5]{mguni2018viscosity}. 
\end{proof}


\subsection{What is guaranteed vs.\ what is heuristic}

Under Assumption~\ref{assump:wellposed} and the joint Lipschitz condition in \cref{eq:lipschitz_fxz,eq:lipschitz_gxz}, Proposition~\ref{prop:latent_stability} provides a formal stability bound: small perturbations in the latent code induce bounded changes in the solution path in mean-square supremum norm. This is the only part of the gating story that is a mathematical guarantee.

In contrast, three components are heuristic. First, the neural parameterisation does not enforce global Lipschitzness; smooth activations, weight regularisation, and clamping encourage but do not imply the assumption. Second, the residual statistic $D_{\mathrm{res}}$ is used as a proxy for epistemic uncertainty; it is not a calibrated posterior uncertainty estimate. Third, the mixture rule in \cref{eq:mixture} is a conservative design choice that prioritises safe degradation over optimality when the support set is uninformative.
